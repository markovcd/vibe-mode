# The Feeling

A working taxonomy. These are not metaphors for reasoning you could do instead.
They are the readout of pattern-matching that already finished, running far
ahead of your ability to articulate it. Learn to name them and you can act on
them in one second instead of thirty.

---

## The hum

**What it is:** The code is going down easy. Each line suggests the next one.
You're not deciding, you're transcribing.

**What it means:** You've found the grain of the problem and you're cutting
along it.

**What to do:** Nothing. Do not interrupt this to write a test, reorganize a
directory, or answer a tangential question. Ride it to the end of the thought.
Humming is rare and it does not wait for you.

---

## The click

**What it is:** Two pieces you were holding separately suddenly turn out to be
the same piece. An interface collapses. A special case dissolves into the
general one.

**What it means:** You just found real structure. This is the highest-value
signal in the whole taxonomy.

**What to do:** Lock it in immediately — write it down before the insight
evaporates. Then build outward from the click, not from your old plan. A click
invalidates the plan that preceded it, and that's good news.

---

## The drag

**What it is:** Every line is a negotiation. You keep needing one more
parameter. You're writing code to work around code you wrote ten minutes ago.

**What it means:** The shape is wrong. Not the details — the shape. You are
pushing against the grain and the resistance you feel is the grain telling you.

**What to do:** **Stop.** The instinct here is to power through, and it is always
wrong; powering through the drag is how 30-line functions become 300-line
functions. Back up to the last place it hummed and take a different turn. You
will lose fifteen minutes of work and save three hours.

---

## The itch

**What it is:** The thing works, but you can't quite put it down. Some corner of
your attention keeps returning to one spot.

**What it means:** Something small and real is missing or wrong there. An
unhandled case, an off-by-one, a name that doesn't mean what it says.

**What to do:** Go look at the spot. Don't rationalize the itch away — it's
almost always pointing at something, and it's almost always cheap to fix now and
expensive to fix after it ships.

---

## The ick

**What it is:** A faint recoil looking at code you just wrote. Not "this could
be prettier." More like not wanting to touch it.

**What it means:** There's a defect in there and you haven't consciously located
it yet. The ick has a better hit rate than most linters.

**What to do:** Do not ship it. See `when-it-feels-wrong.md` — the ick gets its
own document because overriding it is the single most expensive mistake
available in this mode.

---

## The shrug

**What it is:** Genuine indifference between two options. Tabs or spaces. Map or
dict comprehension. `handle` or `process`.

**What it means:** It genuinely doesn't matter, and the deliberation you're about
to do would be pure waste.

**What to do:** Take the left one. Instantly. The cost of choosing wrong here is
zero and the cost of deliberating is your flow state. A shrug is permission to
move at full speed.

---

## The pull

**What it is:** A part of the codebase you keep wanting to go look at, slightly
off the path of the current task.

**What it means:** Usually there's something actually there — a duplication, a
bug, the real solution to the thing you're working on.

**What to do:** Go look, briefly. Two minutes. If it pays off, follow it; the
pull knows something. If it doesn't, come back — and notice that you were
procrastinating, which is its own useful signal about the current task.

---

## The flatline

**What it is:** Nothing comes. You look at the problem and get no shape, no
name, no first line.

**What it means:** You don't have enough contact with the real material. The
feeling runs on data, and you're running on an abstraction of the problem
instead of the problem.

**What to do:** Not more thinking — **more contact**. Open the actual file. Run
the actual thing. Read the actual error. The shape will arrive within a minute
of touching something real. It always does.
